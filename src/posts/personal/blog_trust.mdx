Trust has this catch-22 problem that you can only know if you can trust someone if you trust them in the first place. We want proof that we can trust someone before we can trust them, yet in order to obtain that proof we need to trust them in the first place. It's because we are trying to prove a negative. We want evidence that they will never break our trust, yet as we all know you cannot prove a negative<Footnote presentation="0">When renting my flat in Korea I was asked to prove I was not a sex worker. I looked at them dumbfounded as to how they expect me to prove that. The estate agent that was working on my side told me to just send them my LinkedIN. But not only could I say anything on there, just because I had worked as a software engineer, doesn't mean I couldn't have moonlighted as a sex worker. I don't. But there's a reason the saying goes "Innocent until proven guilty.".</Footnote>. 

However, because we want this proof that someone is not going to hurt us before we do, we put up these walls around us that do not come down. They don't come down, because they can't prove it, so we never have a chance to let them in. In the end, relationships remain superficial. 

## The Walls: Performed Vulnerability
Over time I had developed my most unbreakable wall of all. I call it performative vulnerability. I would be able to talk about and disclose things to people most people would shy away from ever revealing to anyone. But thing was I had intellectualised every single thing I was saying. It had no emotional impact to me so I wasn't truly being vulnerable. Because as far as I could tell, there was no negative way for them to use the information I was giving them. 

While I don't know if they could sense it, despite logically thinking I was being very vulnerable and authentic, I know that for me this still created a distance between me and them that could not be crossed. I was performing trust, not actually trusting them. So I didn't get hurt, because they never actually got near a point where they could hurt me. 

## Risk of Trust: Feeling Stupid

I have seen it in myself and in others how capable we are to recover from being hurt. It may be hard, but eventually we are able to deal with the consequences of putting our trust in the wrong person. I have seen it in myself. I have seen it in others. The one thing I have noticed, however, that seems to be the last to go away is the feeling of "being stupid" for having trusted them. And that seems to be the most common feeling that prevents trusting others in the future. The "I should have known better" feeling. 

Here's the thing, though. By placing the blame on ourselves, the guilt we feel for seeing the signs and not getting out sooner, or not recognising the signs until it was too late, or just for trusting them too soon, by some definition there of... we claim some control over the belief that we could have prevented it. And that belief makes us work even harder to prevent it in the future, ultimately creating a disconnect between us and the others. Because "I was stupid" puts the responsibility on us to get smarter.

But here's the thing... Even if we did see it, we gave them a chance. That was a choice. Because that's what good people do. Because it could have been a mistake, a misunderstanding, etc... It's not stupidity, it's kindness. And if you ask me, the world could do with a lot more of that.

## Is It Really Naiveté?  

There are many ways to break someone's trust. It could be intentional through manipulation, it could be callous through negligence and lack of care for the other person, or it could be accidental but lacking accountability and desire to repair the relationship. But often we can't help but feel naive when we fall for someone's lies. Even when they were the ones that lied. 

Of course, there aren't many people who would give their house keys to a complete strangers. But that doesn't mean bad actors can't find ways to convince them to give them their keys by using multiple persuasion tools. We see this happen often in victims of scams. While there are ways to protect oneself from scams, even greatly intelligent people, some who work in scam detection have been known to fall for them. The feeling of being stupid after falling for the scam is the more dangerous one, as it leads the victims to not speak up about the crime, out of shame. Sometimes, even worse, it enables them to be even further manipulated, as their brain identifies the risk of feeling stupid as worse than whatever danger the bad actor poses. 

## Trust Yourself Above All Else

In my experience when my mind failed my body didn't. Lies and manipulations often make logical sense and are meant to move our brains. But our body doesn't care about all that. And when we are in unsafe positions it starts to react. Without logical reason, because it doesn't need one. It may look like being unable to sleep, it may look like like IBS, it may look like a complete loss of libido. It's the change that usually is the first warning sign, but ignore it for long enough it will start feeling like it was always like that, and we begin to ignore the signs our body is giving us. Because it will likely not be an abrupt all of a sudden change.

Gaslighting works because we do not fully trust ourselves, or we trust the other person more than we trust ourselves. It's not a bug, it's our feature. The way we perceive the world is inevitably going to be biased. So being able to take the input of other people helps us get a better understanding of the world, and know things we would need multiple lifetimes to be able to experience all ourselves. 

There is an additional challenge however. Over time however we build defenses around being right, for some stronger than others. We build images of ourselves, and when we are presented with something that would make that image untrue, we instinctively try an protect that image, because it threatens our existence as we have known it this far. We sometimes care more about being right than doing what is good for us. I have seen people sabotage their job because it was more important to them that they were right, than them doing a good job at the end. But there are signs. There is always discomfort. If someone makes fun of you for having blue hair, and you don't have blue hair, there is no discomfort, because it just isn't true. Our bodies are often our best warning signals. Because they don't overthink, they don't rationalise. If we're afraid, we lose our appetite. If we're anxious, we overeat to self soothe. If we feel unsafe around someone, we physically shut down around them, tense up... If we trust those signals, we can act on them more reliably than we can on mere words.
